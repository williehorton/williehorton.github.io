01. Scheduled agents, Airtable, Google Docs

Bid Radar and Bid Desk

An environmental services company qualified for public-sector contracts but rarely saw them. Solicitations were spread across a dozen procurement sites, and checking them was nobody's assigned job. A dozen portals and thirty search terms every week is more than one person can keep up with alongside a sales role.

I built two scheduled agents. Bid Radar runs every Monday morning, searches Portland and Seattle procurement sites, grades each solicitation A, B, or C against the company's services, and writes it to Airtable. Bid Desk runs every weekday, drafts a response package as a Google Doc, and uses only company facts a person has marked verified. Neither agent can submit, send, or set a price. The company reviews a graded list each Monday instead of searching sites; bid decisions stay with the company.

How it keeps itself honest

  • Each opportunity is graded A, B, or C against the company's service lines, so the reviewer reads 40 items instead of 136.

Lines the AI cannot cross

  • No invented company facts. License numbers, insurance limits, and past-performance claims come only from rows marked verified.
  • No pricing. Pricing is judgment and liability. It stays with a person.
  • No sending. The agent writes drafts. A person hits send.

What broke

02. Decision logic: one design, three implementations

Follow-up priority logic that computes who to call first

One salesperson with about forty open conversations and no system for follow-up order. Leads went cold not because they were difficult but because they arrived on busy days and nothing tracked who had waited longest.

I designed the priority logic first, as a written specification: pipeline stage, last inbound message, last outbound message, and a one-hour first-response standard feed one formula that sorts the list each morning. It was built first as an Airtable CRM with automatic email intake, then carried into a follow-up dashboard built on Lovable and Supabase, and the stage logic now also drives the company's lead system through a Calendly webhook. The tools changed; the rules did not. There is no AI in it: priority is a rule, and a rule gives the same answer every time.

03. Product build: React, design system, rules engine

Cadence, a daily planner that holds you to a standard

My sales role has a written performance standard: estimates entered within 24 hours, leads reviewed on set days, a daily check-in, a monthly sales target. Standards in a memo are easy to lose track of. I wanted mine on the same screen where I plan my day, with the checks built in.

Cadence is a daily planner, modeled on Sunsama, with the standard built in as rules. Rule items appear on the day board automatically from stored data. A completion guard reverts any check-off whose required fields are empty, so a task cannot be marked done until the required information is filled in. A scheduled morning job loads the email list from a task database, read-only. Colors, spacing, and type are defined once as tokens and measured from real product stylesheets.

How it was built

  • A written build contract is the single source of truth. It names the foundation files (store, types, tokens, design system) that cannot be changed without an explicit decision.
  • Design research first: the real production stylesheet of the reference app was checked in and measured, and the restyle was done against those values. Fonts are self-hosted; motion runs on one shared duration and easing.
  • Feature modules include accessibility, undo, keyboard shortcuts, drag and drop, search, and theming. The build must pass before any commit.
What is missing, handover

What is missing

  • No automated tests yet. The completion guard and the importer are the two places that need them first, and they are next.
  • Single user by design. Multi-user would mean a backend and an auth model I have deliberately not built.

Handover

  • The contract and a short handoff document describe the architecture, the rules, and the open items. Anyone who reads them can pick up the next commit.

The code is private. I am glad to walk through the board, the guard, and the morning feed on a call.

04. Grounded generation, catalog constraints

An estimate drafter that cannot invent a line item

As account manager and estimator for a Portland general contractor, I wrote thirty to forty proposals a month. A bathroom remodel estimate could take an afternoon, most of it looking up catalog items, entering them, and doing the arithmetic by hand. That year the company grew from $1.3M to $3.6M in gross revenue, and estimating was the bottleneck.

I built a prompt that turns scope notes and measurements into a line-item proposal using only the company's approved Zoho item catalog. Project management and mobilization are fixed percentages. If the work needs an item not in the catalog, the draft says so and stops. Every line prints its dimensions, its source, and its math so a reviewer can check it quickly. The estimator's job became reviewing a draft instead of building one.

Role
Estimator; built it, used it
Catalog
Exact item IDs, names, rates only
Fixed math
PM 3.123%, mobilization 3.43%
Output
Staged, confirmed section by section, then JSON

What the prompt enforces

  1. Items come only from the approved catalog export, by exact ID, name, and rate. No synonyms, no near matches.
  2. Labor and material quantities are paired per line; a material line without its labor line is an error.
  3. Project management is 3.123% of materials, sub labor, sub materials, and finish materials. Mobilization is 3.43% of the labor subtotal. Both fixed.
  4. Permits, engineering, and plans appear only when the scope requests them.
  5. Each section is drafted, then confirmed by the estimator, before the next section is drafted.
  6. Every line prints what is happening and why, the dimensions and measurements, where each number came from, and the arithmetic.
  7. Final output is customer-keyed JSON for the estimating system, produced only after approval.

The rule set from the prompt, as specified in September 2025.

Lines the AI cannot cross

  • Nothing outside the catalog, ever.
  • No permits, engineering, or plans unless asked.
  • No hidden calculations. "Show the math" is a hard rule.
What I learned, handover

What I learned

  • The model is good at matching work to catalog items and writing descriptions, and unreliable at arithmetic across a long proposal. Requiring it to show the math made the output checkable. I apply the same rule in every system I build.

Handover

  • The prompt is one document with its rules at the top. Anyone with the catalog export can run it.

On request: one AI-drafted estimate beside the corrected version, math visible.

05. Document standardization agent, automated QA: 124 documents delivered

An agent that standardizes pharmacy prior-authorization criteria for a major healthcare provider

A regional health plan's pharmacy team needed one formatted prior-authorization criteria document per drug, assembled from a template, a published criteria PDF, and a quantity-limit list. Formatting had to match the template exactly and criteria wording had to be verbatim, because these documents inform coverage decisions. The agent never writes a document from scratch; it edits a copy of the approved template and every edit is checked before a person sees the result.

The agent extracts criteria word for word and runs eight automated checks after every build, from template preservation at the byte level to a cross-check of each approval duration against its source sentence. If any check fails, the document is not shown. It works from published criteria only, uses no patient data, and is separated from every other client's files. A batch of 33 drugs is delivered as 33 documents that have already passed all checks, ready for one review.

Role
Sole builder, contract engagement
Delivered
124 documents, 6 batches, Aug to Oct 2026
QA
8 automated checks; 124 of 124 pass

Published criteria, no patient data

The eight checks, from the builder's QA script

  1. check_template_preservation: the output's archive members match the template byte for byte except the parts the builder is allowed to touch.
  2. check_container_integrity: the .docx container is structurally valid after surgery.
  3. check_sectpr: section properties, page size, and margins identical to the template.
  4. check_table_and_title: the criteria table matches the template except the two changed cells; title formatting untouched.
  5. check_numbering_refs: list numbering is additive only; original definitions untouched, each added list a faithful clone.
  6. check_content: no placeholder text, no invented identifiers, every criteria line present verbatim, every approval duration cross-checked against its own source sentence.
  7. check_footer: Revised and Effective dates landed where the template expects them, or were deliberately blanked.
  8. check_python_docx: the document reopens cleanly in a second library with the fields that must be blank confirmed blank.

Any failed check blocks the document. The script gates on exit codes, not log text, so a crash cannot be mistaken for a pass.

Lines the AI cannot cross

  • No clinical inference. No invented identifiers, no assumed approval duration, no paraphrased criteria.
  • No sending. Output lands in a review folder.
  • No contact with any other client's files or agents.
What broke, handover

What broke

  • A QA check had its own bug. The approval-duration test hardcoded the phrase "for 12 months," so it only ever confirmed that 12-month pathways were 12-month pathways. A drug with a 24-month pathway failed it, and the failure was in the check, not the document. I rewrote it to compare against whatever duration the source declares, which also catches the reverse error the old version could never see: a source stating a duration the document omits.
  • Rules that lived only in a working session faded between sessions, and the agent drifted back to default behavior. Every rule now lives in the definition the agent reads on every run, with a backup saved before each policy change.

What maturity looks like

  • The sixth batch of 33 drugs needed no new QA rules; existing checks handled everything it raised. Where a source document was malformed (a date with three digits in the day field), the agent transcribed it as written and flagged it rather than guessing a correction.

Handover

  • A README documents the writer strategy and every QA check. Adding a drug means adding one data file. Shared code never changes.

Client documents are not shown. The QA checks above are the public record of how each one was verified.

06. Research bench: multi-agent orchestration

Luna, a personal multi-agent system used as a test bench

A dedicated Mac mini runs a small set of named agents under one governance document, handling my own calendar, mail, and notes. I use it to test approval, isolation, and monitoring patterns before using them in client work.

Each agent proposes an action; nothing runs until I approve it by text message. Current jobs: a morning digest of commitments pulled from mail and meeting notes, and family calendar reminders sent after I approve them. Agents run as a non-admin user on a separate Apple ID, with heartbeat monitoring and a written restart procedure. No agent gets a self-improvement loop until it has a test set to grade itself against.

Defaults, and what would change them

These are starting positions, not fixed rules. Each comes from a cost I have seen, and each can change when the situation shows a different cost.

  • A person reads anything customer-facing before it goes out. The cost of one wrong email to a client is higher than the cost of a review step. When a message type has run clean for months and the downside of a miss is small, the review can become a sample instead of a gate.
  • Pricing, approvals, and money movement start as rules and people, not models. A rule can be audited and explained; a model's answer today may differ tomorrow. If a business has a pricing policy clear enough to write as a formula, the model can fill in the inputs and the formula can do the math.
  • Systems run on the organization's accounts, with documentation for whoever comes next. I hold this one firmly, because the alternative leaves a company dependent on one person.
  • If a formula can answer it, start with the formula. AI belongs where the input is language or judgment, not arithmetic. When inputs are messy, the model's job is to clean them up before the formula runs.
  • Map how the work actually flows, including exceptions, before choosing a tool. For a small, reversible build, a quick prototype can be cheaper than analysis, and the prototype becomes the discovery.

How I build

  1. Draft first; a person approves.The system prepares the work; a person signs off. This limits liability and is what makes it acceptable to put automation next to customer email, contracts, and regulated documents. Autonomy is extended one task type at a time, based on track record.
  2. Rules for the math, AI for the text.Priority, pricing, deadlines, and permissions are formulas and code. The model reads, classifies, and writes. It does not do arithmetic.
  3. Small first version, then expand from real use.Build the smallest version that helps, keep a raw copy of every input, and add features only where use shows they are needed.
The build is about a third of the work. The rest is writing the procedure, training the people who will use it, and checking at thirty days whether they are. I have rolled out CRMs, onboarding programs, and reporting to retail sales teams, so the adoption part is familiar.
